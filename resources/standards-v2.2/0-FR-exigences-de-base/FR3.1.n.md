## Clarifying the compliance criteria

- The scope is the company's own operations
- Litigation: The process of resolving disputes through the court system. It involves the filing of a lawsuit, legal proceedings, and judicial decisions.
- Penalties: A sanction imposed as a consequence of violating laws, regulations, or contractual obligations. It can be financial (such as fines), operational (such as restrictions or suspensions), or other forms of punishment designed to deter non-compliance and enforce rules.
- Public allegation: A material and credible statement that an individual or organization has engaged in illegal or unethical activities.
- In FR3, the scope of "material and credible statement" is limited to statements from:
  - news organizations
  - civil society organizations
  - governments
  - open letters or petitions with five or more named signatures.
- The scope does not include standalone social media posts. Social media posts are in scope if they are referenced in statements from news organizations, civil society organizations, governments, or open letters or petitions with five or more named signatures.
- Misleading marketing: False or deceptive information, or important information is left out. It covers claims made to consumers by manufac-turers, distributors or retailers. It can include content in packaging, ads, catalogues, websites, social media, etc.
- Greenwashing: The practice of making environmental claims about a product, service, or organization that are misleading, unsubstantiated, vague, or disproportionate to the actual environmental impact or benefits, especially when these claims are not backed by scientific evidence and independent verification, or when they obscure trade-offs and negative impacts.
- Key elements of greenwashing include the below.
  - lack of verification: Claims are not independently verified by an accredited third party.
  - no scientific basis: Claims are not based on recognized scientific evidence or technical standards.
  - disproportionate or selective: A claim focuses on a small green aspect while ignoring larger negative environmental impacts (e.g. claiming “sustainable packaging” while the product itself is highly polluting).
  - vague or generic: Terms like “eco-friendly” or “green” are used without clear definitions or substantiation.
  - omission of trade-offs: The claim hides significant negative environmental impacts elsewhere in the product's lifecycle.
  - non-comparability: Claims cannot be fairly compared to similar products or services. (adapted from EU Green Claims Directive)
- Examples of misleading marketing or greenwashing include:
  - a false claim or impression about the details of a good or service (e.g. overstating the performance or qualities of the product, or highlighting one positive aspect, like plastic-free packaging, while omitting others, like water pollution or poor working conditions)
  - misrepresenting the price (e.g. advertising a product at sale price when it is not on sale)
  - misrepresenting consumer rights (e.g. saying consumer rights do not apply during a sale). (adapted from Competition and Consumer Protection Commission - Ireland)
